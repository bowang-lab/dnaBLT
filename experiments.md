# Experiments



## Evaluation Metrics and Protocol

We will use both **quality metrics** and **efficiency metrics** to evaluate the models:

* **Token-level Perplexity (PPL):** Our primary quality metric is perplexity on a held-out test set of genome sequences. We compute cross-entropy loss on the next-nucleotide prediction and report perplexity = exp(loss). Lower perplexity indicates better predictive modeling of genomic sequences. We will report average perplexity across the test set for each model/experiment, as is standard in language model evaluation. Perplexity will be measured for full-length sequences (e.g. contiguous genome segments) to capture long-range dependencies. We will particularly highlight perplexity improvements of BLT+Hyena2 over Evo (baseline) at equal model size and training budget.

* **Inference Latency:** We measure the time taken to produce outputs, specifically **per-token latency** (e.g. milliseconds per generated nucleotide) and **end-to-end sequence latency** for processing a full context. Latency is measured on a standardized hardware setup with a fixed batch size. We will compare latency as a function of sequence length, expecting StripedHyena2 to have significantly lower growth in latency with context length due to its sub-quadratic time complexity (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.15794).



All metrics will be averaged over multiple runs or seeds where appropriate to ensure statistical significance. We will use standard deviations or confidence intervals to report variability. Where relevant, we will follow evaluation protocols from prior genomic language model papers (e.g. using the **Nucleotide Transformer** benchmark suite for regulatory element prediction, as in HyenaDNA).


## Scaling Experiments (4 FLOP Levels, Varied Token\:Parameter Ratios)

**Experiment 1: Scaling Law Analysis.** We will conduct a scaling study across **four different compute budgets (FLOP levels)** to characterize how the new architecture’s performance scales with model size and data, in line with mechanistic scaling law approaches. At each budget, we will train a model from scratch and measure its achieved perplexity. The four budgets will range from **small-scale** to **large-scale**. The **token\:parameter ratio** (amount of training data per model parameter) will be systematically varied across these runs:

* **Compute-Optimal Training:** For one run at each model size, we will allocate training tokens following the compute-optimal paradigm (roughly the Chinchilla strategy). 

* **Undertrained vs Overtrained Regimes:** At two of the four scales (e.g. the smallest and one mid-sized model), we will also train models with **off-optimal** token counts to probe scaling behavior. Specifically, we will **overtrain** a smaller model (providing substantially more tokens than compute-optimal for that size) and **undertrain** a larger model (fewer tokens than optimal) while keeping total compute similar. This yields different token\:parameter ratios. We will measure the **perplexity gap** when training off the optimal frontier, as in recent analyses. According to mechanistic scaling laws, hybrid models often have **flatter** perplexity curves off-optimal (smaller loss increase when overtrained) compared to Transformers. We will verify if BLT+Hyena2 exhibits a smaller optimality gap, indicating it is more robust to overtraining or undertraining than the baseline.

* **Scaling Curves:** For each architecture (BLT+Hyena2 and baseline Evo/Transformer), we will plot **log-perplexity vs log-model-size** and fit power-law curves. We expect to find a lower perplexity for the new model at equal size, and possibly a different scaling exponent. If StripedHyena2 improves efficiency, the new model may attain the same perplexity as a baseline model with significantly fewer parameters or FLOPs. We will also identify the **“break-even” point** where the new architecture overtakes the baseline (e.g. perhaps even a smaller BLT+Hyena2 might match a larger Evo model’s perplexity).

We probe **compute-optimal, under-trained, and over-trained runs at four FLOP budgets** because a single “best-tuned” curve hides two facts reviewers care about: (1) **true efficiency**—where each architecture sits on the loss-vs-compute frontier—and (2) **robustness to real-world mis-allocation of tokens.** By sandwiching every compute point with a run that gets too few tokens and one that gets too many, we map the full envelope of achievable perplexities. If BLT + StripedHyena-2 stays close to its optimal line while Evo’s perplexity rises sharply off-ridge, we demonstrate that the new hybrid is not only *more compute-efficient* at the sweet spot but also *more forgiving* when practitioners inevitably over-train or under-train.


## Latency Experiments

Latency and Parallelism: We will measure the latency per generation step for long sequences. Since BLT processes in two stages (local byte encoder/decoder and global latent model), we’ll measure the overall end-to-end latency to predict the next token for a given context. StripedHyena2 layers operate in parallel across sequence positions (like convolution), so the new model can leverage GPU parallelism better than the sequential attention mechanism. We will compare the time to generate, say, 1000 new tokens from a context of 100k for each model. If BLT+Hyena2 can utilize longer patch sizes or convolutional kernel windows, it may require fewer sequential operations than the baseline, yielding lower latency per token. 


## Bio Evals

To convincingly show that the new BLSH2 architecture outperforms the original Evo model, we should do a focused set of evaluation experiments from the Evo 2 paper. These cover diverse biological modalities (DNA, RNA, protein) and task types (fitness prediction, splicing, generation) while remaining computationally feasible. Each evaluation is described below with its type (zero-shot or supervised), dataset, metric, and rationale:

1. **ClinVar Pathogenic Variant Prediction (Zero-Shot)** – *Zero-shot evaluation on human variants.* Use the ClinVar database of human genetic variants labeled as pathogenic or benign. For each variant (e.g. a single-nucleotide change), compute the model’s log-likelihood for the reference vs. the mutant sequence; the difference serves as a zero-shot pathogenicity score. **Metric:** Report classification accuracy via AUROC distinguishing pathogenic vs. benign variants. This task tests how well each model has learned evolutionary constraints that make mutations deleterious. A higher AUROC for BLSH2 would demonstrate superior inherent ability to identify disease-causing mutations. (For reference, figure 3)

2. **SpliceVarDB Splicing Variant Effect Prediction (Zero-Shot)** – *Zero-shot evaluation on splicing outcomes.* Use SpliceVarDB, a curated set of human genetic variants (both exonic and intronic) with experimentally validated effects on mRNA splicing. The model scores each variant by the drop in sequence likelihood when the mutation is introduced into its genomic context (zero-shot, no task-specific training). **Metric:** AUROC for classifying whether a variant disrupts normal splicing vs. neutral. This evaluation focuses on RNA-level consequences and tests if the model understands splice signals (e.g. canonical splice sites, branch points). (For reference, figure 3d)

3. **Protein Fitness Prediction via Deep Mutational Scanning (Zero-Shot)** – *Zero-shot correlation with protein fitness assays.* Leverage multiple deep mutational scanning (DMS) datasets, where hundreds of single amino-acid substitutions in a protein (e.g. BRCA1 or various enzymes) have experimentally measured activity or fitness scores. For each mutant, use the model’s sequence likelihood (or likelihood ratio relative to wild-type) as a zero-shot fitness prediction, and compare to the experimental value. **Metric:** Spearman rank correlation between the model’s scores and the measured fitness across mutations. This tests the model’s ability to capture protein structure-function constraints purely from its training (protein modality). (For reference, figure 2a & 2b)

4. **Exon/Intron Classification with Embeddings (Supervised probe)** – *Lightweight supervised test of learned DNA features.* Extract sequence embeddings from each model and train a simple classifier to label genomic positions as **exonic** or **intronic** based on reference gene annotations. For example, follow Evo 2’s approach: train a small neural network on embeddings from each model, using known exon/intron labels from a few species, then evaluate on held-out species. **Dataset:** Gene annotations (e.g. GENCODE or RefSeq) across multiple genomes; **Metric:** Area Under ROC for classifying exonic base-pairs in test genomes. This task evaluates the richness of the model’s learned representation of genome sequence features (it spans DNA modality and gene-structure knowledge). A better architecture should produce embeddings that separate exons from introns with higher accuracy. 

5. **Genome-Scale Sequence Generation (Zero-Shot Generation)** – *Unsupervised genome sequence generation and quality assessment.* Have each model generate long DNA sequences (hundreds of kilobases to megabase) such as an entire small genome or a large chromosome segment, given a short seed prompt. Evaluate the biological realism of the generated sequences by comparing against real genomes. **Metrics:** Report quantitative measures of “naturalness,” for example the fraction of generated open reading frames that have homology to known proteins (e.g. Pfam domain hits), the distribution of genomic elements (genes, tRNAs, repeats) in the synthetic sequence vs. a real genome, and protein structural metrics. 

Each of the above evaluations yields a clear quantitative outcome (correlation, AUROC, or percentage) that can directly compare BLSH2 to Evo. By covering variant effect prediction in coding DNA (ClinVar pathogenicity), RNA splicing (SpliceVarDB), and protein domains (DMS fitness), plus a gene-structure classification probe and a genome generation test, we ensure a broad demonstration of BLSH2’s superior performance across the central dogma. These experiments are largely zero-shot or require minimal fine-tuning, making them feasible to implement, and they were pivotal in the Evo 2 study for establishing the model’s state-of-the-art capabilities. Together, this subset of evaluations will provide compelling evidence that the BLSH2 architecture delivers tangible improvements in biological sequence understanding and generation over the Evo model.
